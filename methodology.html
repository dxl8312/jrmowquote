Methodology & Sources

Every number in our calculator is traceable. This page explains the formula and cites the exact public data we used — so you (and search engines) can verify it.

⚠️
Estimates for planning only — never a binding quote. Figures are built from published 2026 market benchmarks and climate data, then run through a transparent formula that includes a size-efficiency curve (section 2). State and provincial rates are cost-of-living proxies, not surveyed lawn-care prices — see the confidence levels in section 5. Your real price depends on your exact lawn, access, and local competition. Always confirm with a local provider before you buy.

1. The complete formula

The calculator applies a small set of transparent multipliers to a size-adjusted anchor rate, then adds any selected services. This is the exact order of operations in index.html — nothing is hidden. The arithmetic path is identical for every region; what differs is the provenance of the inputs: the 51 US indices are reproducible from the published Q3 2026 MERIC/C2ER table, while the 13 Canadian indices are derived from Statistics Canada's 2021 household-consumption purchasing power parities, re-based to our own population-weighted national average (sections 4–6).

Step 0 — the size-efficiency factor

sizeFactor(A) = Math.pow( Math.max(A, 1000) / 5000, −0.35 )  where A is lawn area in sq ft. If you need to work out that area first, the lawn area calculator converts length × width — or a rough outline — into sq ft, sq yd and acres.

This runs before the job minimum and before every other multiplier. It is the reason a 1-acre lawn prices at about $235 rather than the $501 a flat $0.0115/sq ft would produce. Section 2 publishes the curve in full.

Step 1 — per-visit mowing price

mowPerVisit = max( A × anchorRate × sizeFactor(A), baseMin ) × condition × (1 + terrain) × (1 + edgeAdj) × (1 − planDiscount) × regionIndex

  • anchorRate — $0.0115/sq ft (US) or CA$0.011/sq ft (Canada), at the 5,000 sq ft anchor only. This is not a flat rate: the effective rate declines with area because of sizeFactor.
  • baseMin — $35 job minimum (CA$35 × 1.3 FX), applied after the size factor. Small lawns are priced by the fixed call-out cost, not by area.
  • condition — grass height/state: short 0.9 · normal 1.0 · tall 1.45 · overgrown 1.9.
  • terrain — flat +0% · gentle +10% · moderate +20% · steep +35%.
  • edgeAdj — by default the obstacle bucket: none +0% · moderate +15% · heavy +30%. If you enter a measured perimeter P, it replaces the bucket with clamp( (P / (4√A) − 1) × 0.5, 0, 0.45 ), capped at +45%. A square lot has perimeter ≈ 4√A, so a longer, more intricate boundary adds trimming time.
  • planDiscount — weekly −15% · bi-weekly −10% · monthly −5% · one-time 0% (recurring saves the crew routing time).
  • regionIndex — US: published MERIC/C2ER cost-of-living index ÷ 100 (reproducible from the official table). Canada: Statistics Canada 2021 household-consumption PPP ÷ its 2021-population-weighted national mean (our modelling choice; official base is Ontario = 1.00), rounded to 3 decimals. Either way this is a price-level proxy, not a surveyed lawn-care price — and the Canadian figure is 2021 data, not a 2026 lawn-service index — see sections 4–5.

Step 2 — recurring vs one-time

  • One-time: annual cost = per-visit price (1 visit).
  • Recurring: visits per year = visitsPerMonth × seasonMonths; monthly = per-visit × visitsPerMonth; annual = monthly × seasonMonths. So monthly × season = annual always holds.

Step 3 — add-on services

svcPrice = max( rate × A × sizeFactor(A), min × fx ) × (1 + terrain) × (1 + edgeAdj) × regionIndex
serviceCost = svcPrice × applicationsPerSeason

Per-sq-ft services use the same size factor as mowing — the earlier version of this page omitted it. Mulch refresh and yard cleanup are quoted flat (flatPrice × fx) instead. fx = 1.3 for Canadian dollars on flat prices and minimums, 1.0 for US dollars.

2. The size-efficiency curve, published in full

Drive time, unloading and setup cost roughly the same on every job. Spreading that fixed cost over more grass is why a big lawn costs less per square foot — and why a single flat $/sq ft rate overprices acreage by about 2×.

The rule: sizeFactor(A) = ( A / 5000 )−0.35 for A ≥ 1,000 sq ft, with A floored at 1,000 below that. The 5,000 sq ft anchor returns exactly 1.000.

  • Why a power curve: it reproduces the published 2026 size bands — roughly $58 at 5,000 sq ft, $90 at 10,000 and $235 at one acre — while keeping the rate smooth with no artificial jumps between size brackets.
  • Why −0.35: it is calibrated so the effective $/sq ft roughly halves between a suburban lot and a full acre. It is a model calibration, not a measured market constant, and we disclose it here so it can be audited, challenged or replaced.
  • Floor at 1,000 sq ft: below this the factor stops rising (1,000 → 1.759). Very small lawns are governed by the $35 minimum anyway.
  • Where the minimum wins: at 2,000 sq ft the curve alone gives $31.70, but the $35 floor applies, so the effective rate is $0.0175/sq ft — higher than the anchor rate. That inversion is real and intentional.

⚠️
Plain statement: we have not collected a single lawn-care invoice, provider quote or platform price for any individual state, city or province. The state/province table is a modelled comparison, not a survey. For the 51 US locations a published cost-of-living index is the public surrogate for local operating costs; for the 13 Canadian locations it is Statistics Canada's 2021 household-consumption PPP. Neither is a lawn-care price index — the Canadian figures are 2021 spending-price levels, not 2026 lawn quotes — and we do not want readers mistaking one for the other.

Why we still use the proxy

  • For the 51 US locations it is public, quarterly and independently reproducible: anyone can rebuild those values from the cited MERIC/C2ER series. The 13 Canadian values are reproducible from the cited Statistics Canada 2021 PPP table (official Ontario = 1.00) once our stated re-basing is applied — dividing by the 2021-population-weighted national mean (0.991746) and rounding to 3 decimals. The PPP itself is official; the population weighting and the choice to use it for mowing are our modelling choices, not a Statistics Canada method.
  • It lets every region be compared on one identical control property. Silently mixing a handful of real city quotes into modelled states would make the ranking unreadable — and, we think, less honest.
  • The common alternative — one national average price applied everywhere — is worse, because it erases the biggest driver of regional variation.

What would make this better, in order

  1. Record provenance for every data point. A standing log with location | service | source | quoted price | lawn size | date collected | sample count | URL. No date and no sample count, no entry.
  2. Collect local provider and platform price ranges for a first batch of 10–20 high-demand cities, split into urban / suburban / rural. Publish the collection date and sample count next to every figure.
  3. Keep those city pages separate from the national model and label them as observed samples. Do not blend observed quotes into the proxy table: a half-surveyed table is harder to audit than an explicitly modelled one.
  4. Back-check every page quoting a changed number — state tables, FAQ, price tables and guides — and log the revision here with its date.
  5. Only use sources we are permitted to cite. Scraped marketplace data may breach a platform's terms; a sample we cannot attribute and date does not enter the model.

Until step 2 produces real samples, treat the state index as directional: good for ranking regions and building a budget range, not for predicting an individual contractor's bid. That is also why the calculator keeps size, condition, terrain, obstacles and frequency as separate inputs — they let your own situation enter the estimate instead of hiding inside a state average.

8. Limitations & how to verify

  • No surveyed lawn-care price data exists in this model. No government publishes a per-state mowing rate, and we have not collected local invoices either. Regional cost is proxied with the Cost of Living Index. Section 5 states this field by field, including the zero sample counts.
  • Cost of living is not a mowing price. It tracks household expenses such as housing, groceries and transport. Within a state, urban cores and resort areas typically run above the index and rural areas below, so one state value cannot describe a zip code.
  • The size curve is a calibration, not a measurement. The −0.35 exponent reproduces the published 2026 size bands; it is published in section 2 so it can be audited, challenged or replaced.
  • Season length is an estimate. It reflects the frost-free growing window, not a surveyed "months people mow." Northern states are conservative.
  • Prices are 2026 snapshots. Marketplace ranges shift with fuel, labour and demand. The last-reviewed date is recorded in section 5.
  • Verify it yourself. Sections 1–3 give a complete arithmetic path from a stated regional input to a price. If a published price cannot be reproduced from those steps, that is a documentation bug — tell us. Reproducing a price from a given multiplier is different from verifying the multiplier's source; see section 5.
  • Best practice: treat the output as a budgeting range, then get 2–3 local quotes. If you operate a lawn business, replace the BASE, REGIONS and SERVICES values in index.html with your own actual pricing — the formula stays valid, and the sizeFactor exponent can be tuned to your own job records.
